No. You can answer a poll or fill in an availability grid without connecting a calendar. A Doodle calendar connection, like the one in any scheduling tool that offers it, is a convenience. It can show your existing events beside the poll and put the chosen meeting on your calendar. Whether that is worth granting access depends on how many polls you answer and how much of your week is on that calendar.

## Two ways a tool learns when you are free

**You tell it.** You look at your own calendar and mark the times you can make. The tool knows only what you marked.

**It reads your calendar.** You grant access once, and the tool can see when you have events.

Both end with a list of times you can make. They differ in effort, in accuracy and in what you share.

A third arrangement sits between them. The tool reads your calendar and shows your events beside the poll, and you still mark the answer yourself. That keeps the decision with you. It still needs the same grant of access, so the questions below apply to it as well.

## What a calendar connection usually does

Scheduling tools use a connected calendar for some or all of these jobs. Which ones a given tool does, and on which plan, is on its own help and pricing pages.

- **Shows your events while you answer.** You see the poll's options next to your day, so you do not switch tabs.
- **Flags conflicts.** An option that overlaps an event is marked for you.
- **Suggests times to the organizer.** The person creating the poll picks options from their own free hours.
- **Creates the event.** When the organizer settles on a time, the meeting lands on the calendar, sometimes with invitations to the participants.
- **Holds the candidate times.** Some tools can place tentative holds on the options until one is chosen.

The first three read from your calendar. The last two write to it. That difference matters when you read the permission screen.

## The trade

|                                     | You mark your free time             | The tool reads your calendar              |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Setup                               | None                                | Grant access once per calendar            |
| Effort per poll                     | Check your calendar, then mark      | Lower. Conflicts can be shown to you.     |
| Covers plans not on the calendar    | Yes. You know about the school run. | No. An empty hour looks free.             |
| Lets you decline a "free" hour      | Yes. You mark only what you want.   | Only if you can override it.              |
| What the tool can see               | The slots you marked                | Whatever the permission you grant allows  |
| Works for people in other companies | Yes                                 | Each person decides for their own account |
| Getting the event on the calendar   | You add it, often from a link       | Often done for you                        |

## How to read the permission screen

When you connect a calendar, your calendar provider shows a consent screen. It lists what the tool is asking for. Read it before you click Allow, because the list is the real answer to "what can this tool see".

- **Read or write.** Reading lets the tool see events. Writing lets it create, change or delete them. A tool that only shows conflicts needs to read. A tool that books the meeting needs to write.
- **Busy times or event details.** Some permissions expose only the blocks of time you are busy. Others expose titles, guests, locations and notes. A title such as "Interview with another company" says more than a grey block does.
- **One calendar or all of them.** An account often holds several calendars: your own, a shared team calendar, a family calendar. Check which ones the grant covers.
- **Other data on the same screen.** A consent screen can bundle calendar access with contacts or profile details. Each line is a separate thing you are agreeing to.

If the screen asks for more than the job needs, you have two honest choices. Decline and mark your times by hand, or accept and know what you shared.

## Questions to ask before you connect

- What does the permission screen list? Read access to event details is more than read access to busy times.
- Does your employer allow third-party apps on the work calendar? Some workspaces require an administrator to approve an app first. Ask before you try, not after.
- Can you disconnect later, and where?
- Is the connection part of the free plan? Plans change, so read the tool's current pricing page. [Doodle free plan or a free Slack poll](/blog/doodle-free-plan-or-a-free-slack-poll) lists what else to check there.
- Which account are you connecting? A work calendar connected to a tool you use for a hobby group mixes two parts of your life.

## Where a connected calendar gets your availability wrong

A calendar is a record of events. It is not a record of when you are willing to meet. These are the common gaps.

- **Plans that were never entered.** The school run, the gym, the commute. An empty hour looks free.
- **Events that do not block you.** An all-day reminder, a colleague's holiday, an optional talk you will skip. The hour looks busy and is not.
- **Tentative events.** A hold you have not accepted may count as busy or as free, depending on the tool.
- **A second calendar.** You connected the work calendar, and the dentist is on the personal one.
- **Time around a meeting.** A meeting across town ends at 2:00. You are not free at 2:00.
- **Hours you protect.** Lunch, or the first hour of the morning. Nothing is on the calendar, and you still do not want a meeting there.

None of these is a fault in any one product. They follow from asking a calendar a question that only a person can answer. A tool that lets you correct its reading before you submit handles them. A tool that answers on your behalf does not.

## A worked example: one poll, answered both ways

A team lead asks six people for a one-hour slot next week and offers five options.

**Without a connection.** Ana opens the poll, opens her calendar in another tab and checks the five options. Two clash with meetings. One is clear on the calendar but falls on the afternoon she collects her child. She marks the remaining two. The job takes a couple of minutes, and the answer is right.

**With a connection.** Ben connected his work calendar last month. The poll shows his events beside the five options, and two are flagged as conflicts. He marks the other three without leaving the page. One of the three overlaps a dentist appointment on his personal calendar, which the tool cannot see. If that option wins, Ben has to ask the group to move it.

The connection saved Ben a tab switch. It also gave him a reason to stop checking. The lesson is not that connecting is wrong. Connect the calendar that holds your real week, and still look at each option before you mark it.

## How to decide

Skip calendar access when any of these is true.

- You answer a few polls a year. The setup costs more than it saves.
- Your calendar does not reflect your real availability.
- The group spans organizations, and half of them will not connect anyway.
- Your employer's policy is unclear and you have not asked.

Marking free time by hand is a small job. It is also the only way to say "that hour is empty and I still do not want a meeting in it".

Connect when all of these are true.

- You organize or answer polls every week.
- Your calendar is accurate and complete.
- The tool shows conflicts and lets you override them.
- The tool creates the event, so the result does not need retyping.

For an organizer, the case is stronger than for a participant. The organizer creates the poll, picks the options and books the result, so a connection saves time at three points. A participant saves time at one.

## Short answers to common questions

### Can the other people in the poll see my events?

A poll is built to share your answer, not your calendar. What a specific tool shows to the organizer or to other participants is a question for its privacy page and help pages. Check there if your event titles are sensitive.

### Do participants have to connect a calendar for the poll to work?

No. The connection is a convenience for each person, and an answer marked by hand counts the same. If you are the organizer, say so in your message. People hesitate when they think a grant of access is required.

A line you can copy:

```text
You do not need to connect a calendar or make an account to answer.
Check your own calendar, then mark every option you can make.
```

### Does connecting mean the tool answers for me?

Not by itself. In most tools the connection informs your answer and you still submit it. If a tool offers to fill in answers from your calendar, read the section above on where a calendar gets it wrong before you turn that on.

### I connected once. How do I undo it?

Disconnect in two places. First, remove the calendar in the scheduling tool's settings. Second, revoke the tool's access in your calendar provider's account settings, on the page that lists third-party apps with access to your account. The second step is the one that ends the access. The first only stops the tool from using it.

### What about a work calendar and a personal one?

Decide which calendar holds the events that would clash with this group's meetings, and connect that one. If the honest answer is "both", and you would rather not connect both, mark by hand.
