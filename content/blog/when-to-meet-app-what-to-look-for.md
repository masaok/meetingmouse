A good when to meet app does three things well. It makes answering quick, it shows every person the times in their own time zone, and it shows the organizer where the group overlaps. Everything else is secondary. The checklist below turns that into questions you can ask of any tool.

## The checklist

| Question                                         | Why it matters                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Can people answer without creating an account?   | A sign-up step is a reason not to answer.                                        |
| Can they mark many slots in one gesture?         | Dragging across a grid is faster than ticking boxes one at a time.               |
| Are times converted for each viewer?             | Without it, people in other zones mark the wrong cells.                          |
| Does it show the overlap, not only the answers?  | The organizer needs the count per slot, not a list of replies to add up by hand. |
| Can people change their answer later?            | Plans move between the ask and the deadline.                                     |
| Where does the result appear?                    | A result on a page nobody revisits still needs someone to announce it.           |
| Does it need access to anyone's calendar?        | Calendar access saves typing and asks for more trust.                            |
| Can people outside your organization answer?     | Clients and candidates will not be in your chat workspace.                       |
| Does it get the meeting onto a calendar?         | The poll is finished only when the event exists.                                 |
| What does it cost, and what is behind the limit? | Read the pricing page for limits on polls and participants.                      |

## Match the tool to the group

The right kind of tool depends on where the people are.

| Your group                                         | A tool that fits                              |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| One team that shares a Slack channel               | A poll that lives in the channel              |
| People from several organizations                  | A web grid shared as a link                   |
| One person offering times to many, one at a time   | A booking page tied to that person's calendar |
| A large group choosing between a few fixed options | A vote on listed options                      |

The difference between the grid and the vote is covered in [When 2 Meet vs Doodle](/blog/when-2-meet-vs-doodle). If you settle on a web grid, [how to make and share a when to meet link](/blog/when-to-meet-link-how-to-make-one-and-share-it) covers the sharing.

## Where Meeting Mouse fits, and where it does not

Meeting Mouse is the first row of that table. It is a Slack app, and against the checklist it looks like this.

- **Accounts.** None. People answer as their Slack user.
- **One gesture.** Partly. The form in Slack is one tick per slot. It links to a page where each person drags across a grid of the same slots.
- **Time zones.** Every time is shown in the viewer's local zone.
- **Overlap.** The channel message shows a grid of who is free when, with the top three slots by headcount above it.
- **Changing an answer.** Click Add my availability again and your earlier answer is already filled in.
- **Where the result appears.** In the channel, in the same message, which updates in place.
- **Calendar access.** None. It does not read calendars. The final announcement includes an Add to Google Calendar link.
- **People who are not in the channel.** This is the gap. A person who is not in the Slack channel cannot answer the poll, so a group that spans organizations is better served by a web grid.
- **Cost.** The hosted app is free while in beta, and the code is open source if you want to run your own copy.
